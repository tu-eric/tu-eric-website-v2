01 / Research

Research & collaboration.

My main research program asks how romantic connections form and develop. I also contribute quantitative analysis to collaborative research on child and adolescent mental health.

My independent research program

Why do some people click?

I investigate how people evaluate potential romantic partners, the experiences that emerge when they interact, and how those experiences inform further romantic pursuit. My research connects relationship theory with measurement, dyadic designs, and prospective data.

01 / Romantic chemistry

Measuring romantic chemistry.

My doctoral research develops and evaluates the Romantic Chemistry (RoCh) Scale in single and partnered community samples. Its 25 items assess compatibility, comfortability, unique connection, positive interactions, and sexual attraction.

Across my dissertation studies, I examine how people describe chemistry, whether partners agree about it, and how it relates to daily relationship experiences and interest after a first date. The goal is to give researchers a clearer way to study an experience people often describe but rarely define.

The development and validation manuscript is available as a preprint and has not yet been peer reviewed.

02 / Dating & partner evaluation

From first impressions to first dates.

People can have clear preferences on paper and still be surprised by their reactions to someone in person. I’m interested in how expectations change as people interact and get to know each other.

My published speed-dating research examines how accurately people perceive others' attachment-related characteristics and how these perceptions relate to dating interest. My planned work tests how chemistry develops across repeated encounters and whether it becomes more informative about pursuit as people get acquainted.

These prospective dating and experimental studies are planned research directions, not completed findings.

03 / Established relationships

Personality and relationship experiences.

My published work also examines charisma, sexual experiences, and conflict within established relationships.

Across five studies, my colleagues and I found that people higher in trait charisma tended to report more constructive conflict strategies and greater relationship satisfaction. Links with partners’ satisfaction were smaller and less consistent. Other collaborations examine sexual beliefs and preferences in relationships.
